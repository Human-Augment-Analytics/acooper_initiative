The Real-World Collaboration Procedure

This is the documented framework derived from the Charleston Trains case study. It describes how HAAG research projects can identify, scope, launch, and sustain real-world partnerships with cities, nonprofits, and organizations.

Who this is for: HAAG faculty and researchers looking to understand the process; administrators managing research collaborations; and external organizations interested in how HAAG approaches partnerships.

Phase 1: Identifying Opportunities

Not every research project is suited for external collaboration—and that's by design. This phase helps determine when a project has genuine partnership potential.

Key Questions

  • Is the research question grounded in a real-world problem? Does it address an authentic challenge faced by a specific community, city, or organization?
  • Would external engagement strengthen the research? Would access to real data, domain expertise, or operational context make the research better?
  • Is there potential for mutual benefit? What does the external partner gain? Is it genuine and substantial?
  • Do we have capacity? Given our distributed model, can we realistically engage with an external partner?
  • Is there a clear champion? Is there a faculty member or researcher personally invested in making this partnership work?

Red Flags (When NOT to Pursue Partnership)

  • The problem is primarily theoretical with no real-world grounding
  • The potential partner wants a solution, not research
  • There's no clear mutual benefit—it's primarily transactional
  • We're pursuing collaboration to secure funding, not because it strengthens research
  • The team is already at capacity with existing commitments

Green Lights (Good Signs for Partnership)

  • A researcher is directly affected by or invested in the problem
  • An external organization has already expressed interest or reached out
  • The research question evolved through conversations with practitioners
  • There's enthusiasm from both sides about what collaboration could achieve
  • The scope is specific and bounded, not vague or expansive

Checkpoint: Go/No-Go Decision

Before investing time in formal conversations, convene the research team and initiative leadership to honestly assess fit. If the answer to most key questions is "yes," you're ready for Phase 2.

Phase 2: Scoping Partnerships

The goal of this phase: define a specific, bounded problem with measurable outcomes and clear expectations.

The Scoping Process

  1. Initial Exploratory Conversation (Weeks 1-2)
    • • Meet with the external partner to understand their challenge from their perspective
    • • Ask questions; don't propose solutions yet
    • • Identify constraints (budget, timeline, political realities, technical limitations)
    • • Look for alignment with HAAG research strengths
  2. Deep Dive Sessions (Weeks 3-6)
    • • Work with the partner to define the specific research question(s)
    • • Identify what data or resources are available
    • • Discuss success metrics—what would success look like?
    • • Understand operational and organizational constraints
  3. Scope Definition Document (End of Week 6)
    • • A 2-3 page document defining the partnership scope
    • • Should include: problem statement, research questions, data/resources needed, success metrics, team structure, timeline, budget, and risks
    • • Both partners review and refine until alignment is clear

Scope Template

Partnership Scope Document should include:

  • • The Problem: What challenge is the partner facing?
  • • Research Questions: What specific questions will the research address?
  • • Scope & Constraints: What's in scope? What's not? Why?
  • • Data & Resources: What data will be used? How is it obtained? What are privacy/security requirements?
  • • Success Metrics: How will we know if the partnership succeeded?
  • • Team Structure: Who's on the research team? Who's the primary contact from the partner?
  • • Timeline: When will different phases happen?
  • • Budget: What are the cost estimates? How is it funded?
  • • Communication Plan: How often will teams meet? What format?

Common Scoping Challenges

Problem: "Partner wants a solution, we want to do research"
Solution: Reframe research as foundation for solutions. Explain that evidence-based recommendations will come from rigorous analysis, not guessing.

Problem: "Timeline is too short for meaningful research"
Solution: Scope a pilot or initial phase. A 3-month exploratory project can establish foundation for longer-term work.

Problem: "Budget is limited"
Solution: Explore grant funding together. Identify what can be done with available resources, what would require external funding.

Checkpoint: Scope Approval

Both partners sign off on the scope document. This is your north star for the partnership—refer back to it constantly and update it if circumstances change.

Phase 3: Building Relationships & Securing Buy-In

Before formal execution, ensure you have institutional support and clear partnership agreements.

Get Institutional Buy-In

  • From HAAG: Faculty advisor, department leadership, and initiative lead should review and approve the scope and partnership plan
  • From External Partner: Not just the champion who initiated contact, but their leadership and relevant departments
  • From Georgia Tech: If IP, data, or liability issues are involved, involve the Office of Technology Transfer or relevant compliance office

Establish Clear Agreements

Work with legal/compliance to document:

  • IP Ownership: Who owns what intellectual property? Can HAAG publish? Under what conditions?
  • Data Handling: How is data secured? Who has access? How is it disposed of at the end?
  • Confidentiality: What information is confidential? For how long?
  • Publication Rights: Can researchers publish? When? Do partners have review rights?
  • Budget & Resources: Who pays for what? What's the cost split?

Build Trust Through Regular Communication

  • Establish a regular meeting cadence (typically monthly check-ins, quarterly deep dives)
  • Create a shared communication channel (email, Slack, etc.)
  • Designate clear points of contact from both sides
  • Be transparent about challenges early—don't wait until they become crises

Checkpoint: Partnership Agreement Signed

Formal agreements are in place. Institutional support is documented. Your team is ready to begin active research.

Phase 4: Executing the Partnership

The research is underway. This phase focuses on maintaining alignment and adapting as understanding evolves.

Best Practices for Research Teams

  • Maintain Regular Communication: Stick to your meeting schedule. Use it for both reporting progress and seeking input on challenges
  • Be Transparent About Challenges: If analysis is taking longer, if data quality is worse than expected, or if assumptions were wrong—say so early
  • Iterate Based on Feedback: Partners often see patterns or implications researchers miss. Incorporate their insights
  • Balance Rigor & Relevance: Academic standards matter, but so does addressing the partner's real constraints
  • Document as You Go: Progress reports, interim findings, lessons learned—keep partners informed in real time

Managing Scope Creep

Partners often want to expand the research or add new questions. This is normal—but protect the original scope:

  • Listen to the request and understand the motivation
  • Be honest about capacity and timeline
  • Consider whether it's a quick addition or substantial new work
  • If substantial, propose it as "Phase 2" with its own scope, timeline, and budget

Handling Difficulties

If data access stalls: Work with the partner to remove barriers. Sometimes it's bureaucratic; sometimes it's political. Help solve it together.

If the problem is more complex than expected: Reframe your research question to match what you can actually address. Better to deliver great findings on a bounded question than mediocre findings on an overambitious one.

If team members change: Document what you know so replacements don't start from scratch. Introduce new team members to partners in person when possible.

Checkpoint: Quarterly Reviews

Every 3 months, step back and assess: Are we on track? Do we need to adjust scope? Are both partners getting value?

Phase 5: Measuring Impact & Knowledge Sharing

As the partnership concludes (or transitions), document what was achieved and share lessons with the broader community.

Define Impact Broadly

Impact isn't just publications. It includes:

  • Research Outcomes: Publications, conference presentations, technical reports
  • Practical Outcomes: Tools deployed, recommendations implemented, processes changed
  • Capacity Building: Partner staff learned new skills; HAAG team gained real-world experience
  • Relationship Outcomes: Strengthened partnership for future collaboration; introductions to other organizations
  • Field Impact: Did this research inform practices in the broader city/domain?

Share What You've Learned

  • Write up the case study for the broader HAAG community
  • Present findings at conferences and in peer-reviewed venues
  • Make tools, data, or code openly available when possible
  • Document lessons learned—what would you do differently next time?

Plan for Continuation or Conclusion

  • If the partnership is ending, create a transition plan so work isn't lost
  • If it's continuing, establish the scope and timeline for the next phase
  • Maintain relationships even if formal partnership ends—these often become valuable in the future

Checkpoint: Impact Documentation

Write up the partnership—what was attempted, what was achieved, what was learned. Share with the HAAG community and broader field.

Typical Partnership Timeline

Weeks 1-2

Exploratory Conversation

Understand the partner's challenge and initial fit assessment.

Weeks 3-6

Deep Scoping

Define research questions, data needs, success metrics.

Weeks 7-10

Build-Out & Agreements

Formalize agreements, get institutional buy-in, align on budget.

Week 12+

Active Research

Execute the partnership with regular communication and iteration.

Note: This timeline assumes a relatively straightforward partnership. Complex projects, regulatory requirements, or governance issues may extend this significantly. The key is not rushing the early phases—investing time in scoping prevents bigger problems later.
