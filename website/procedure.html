The Real-World Collaboration Procedure

This is the documented framework derived from the Charleston Trains case study. It describes how HAAG research projects can identify, scope, launch, and sustain real-world partnerships with cities, nonprofits, and organizations.

Who this is for: Primarily HAAG faculty exploring external partnerships. Also useful for administrators managing research collaborations and external organizations understanding how HAAG approaches partnerships.

Typical Partnership Timeline

Weeks 1-2

Phase 1: Exploratory Conversation

Understand the partner's challenge and assess fit.

Weeks 3-6

Phase 2: Deep Scoping

Define research questions, data needs, and success metrics.

Weeks 7-10

Phase 3: Build-Out & Agreements

Formalize agreements, get institutional buy-in, align on budget.

Week 12+

Phase 4: Active Research

Execute the partnership with regular communication and iteration.

Ongoing

Phase 5: Measuring Impact

Document outcomes, share lessons, and plan for continuation or conclusion.

Note: This timeline assumes a relatively straightforward partnership. Complex projects, regulatory requirements, or governance issues may extend this significantly. The key is not rushing the early phases — investing time in scoping prevents bigger problems later.

Phase 1: Identifying Opportunities

Not every research project is suited for external collaboration. This phase helps determine when a project has genuine partnership potential.

Key Questions

  • Is the research question grounded in a real-world problem? Does it address an authentic challenge faced by a specific community, city, or organization? A purely theoretical problem is a reason to pass.
  • Would external engagement strengthen the research? Would access to real data, domain expertise, or operational context make the research better? Good sign: the research question evolved through conversations with practitioners.
  • Is there genuine mutual benefit? What does the external partner gain? If the collaboration is primarily transactional, or primarily motivated by funding rather than research fit, that's a reason to pass.
  • Do we have capacity? Given our distributed model, can we realistically engage with an external partner? If the team is already at capacity, defer.
  • Is there a clear project leader? A faculty member or researcher who is personally invested and willing to champion the partnership. Strong enthusiasm from both sides is a good sign.

Checkpoint: Go/No-Go Decision

Before investing time in formal conversations, convene the research team and initiative leadership to honestly assess fit. If the answer to most key questions is "yes," you're ready for Phase 2.

Phase 2: Scoping Partnerships

The goal of this phase: define a specific, bounded problem with measurable outcomes and clear expectations.

The Scoping Process

Initial Exploratory Conversation (Weeks 1-2). Meet with the external partner to understand their challenge from their perspective. Ask questions; don't propose solutions yet. Identify constraints around budget, timeline, political realities, and technical limitations, and look for alignment with the team's research strengths.

Deep Dive Sessions (Weeks 3-6). Work with the partner to define specific research questions. Identify what data or resources are available, discuss success metrics, and understand operational and organizational constraints.

Scope Definition Document (End of Week 6). A 2-3 page document defining the partnership scope. It should cover: the problem statement, research questions, data and resources needed, success metrics, team structure, timeline, budget, and risks. Both partners review and refine until alignment is clear.

Scope Template

The Problem: What challenge is the partner facing?

Research Questions: What specific questions will the research address?

Scope & Constraints: What's in scope? What's not? Why?

Data & Resources: What data will be used? How is it obtained? What are the privacy and security requirements?

Success Metrics: How will we know if the partnership succeeded?

Team Structure: Who's on the research team? Who's the primary contact from the partner?

Timeline: When will different phases happen?

Budget: What are the cost estimates? How is it funded?

Communication Plan: How often will teams meet? What format?

Common Scoping Challenges

"Partner wants a solution, not research." Reframe research as the foundation for solutions. Evidence-based recommendations come from rigorous analysis, not guessing.

"Timeline is too short for meaningful research." Scope a pilot or initial phase. A 3-month exploratory project can establish the foundation for longer-term work.

"Budget is limited." Explore grant funding together. Identify what can be done with available resources and what would require external funding.

Checkpoint: Scope Approval

Both partners sign off on the scope document. This is your north star for the partnership. Refer back to it constantly and update it if circumstances change.

Phase 3: Building Relationships & Securing Buy-In

Before formal execution, ensure you have institutional support and clear partnership agreements.

Get Institutional Buy-In

From HAAG: the faculty advisor, OMSCS admin, the project manager and the external collaboration manager should review and approve the scope and partnership plan. From the external partner: not just the champion who initiated contact, but their leadership and relevant departments. From Georgia Tech: if IP, data, or liability issues are involved, bring in the Office of Technology Transfer or the relevant compliance office.

Establish Clear Agreements

Work with legal and compliance to document IP ownership (who owns what, can HAAG publish, under what conditions), data handling (how data is secured, who has access, how it's disposed of at the end), confidentiality terms, publication rights, and the budget and cost split between partners.

Build Trust Through Regular Communication

Establish a regular meeting cadence (typically monthly check-ins and quarterly deep dives), create a shared communication channel, designate clear points of contact from both sides, and be transparent about challenges early rather than waiting until they become crises.

Checkpoint: Partnership Agreement Signed

Formal agreements are in place. Institutional support is documented. Your team is ready to begin active research.

Phase 4: Executing the Partnership

The research is underway. This phase focuses on maintaining alignment and adapting as understanding evolves.

Best Practices for Research Teams

Stick to the meeting schedule and use it for both reporting progress and seeking input on challenges. If analysis is taking longer, data quality is worse than expected, or assumptions were wrong, say so early. Partners often see patterns or implications researchers miss, so incorporate their feedback. Academic standards matter, but so does addressing the partner's real constraints. Keep partners informed in real time through progress reports and interim findings.

Managing Scope Creep

Partners often want to expand the research or add new questions. Listen to the request, understand the motivation, and be honest about capacity and timeline. If it's a small addition, consider accommodating it. If it's substantial new work, propose it as a "Phase 2" with its own scope, timeline, and budget rather than absorbing it into the current project.

Handling Difficulties

Data access stalls: Work with the partner to remove barriers. These can be bureaucratic or political. Solve them together.

Problem more complex than expected: Reframe the research question to match what you can actually address. Focused findings on a bounded question beat overambitious, mediocre ones.

Team members change: Document thoroughly so replacements aren't starting from scratch. Introduce new team members to partners directly.

Checkpoint: Quarterly Reviews

Every 3 months, step back and assess: Are we on track? Do we need to adjust scope? Are both partners getting value?

Phase 5: Measuring Impact & Knowledge Sharing

As the partnership concludes (or transitions), document what was achieved and share lessons with the broader community.

Define Impact Broadly

Impact isn't just publications. It includes research outputs (papers, presentations, technical reports), practical outcomes (tools deployed, recommendations implemented, processes changed), capacity built in both the partner organization and the HAAG team, relationship outcomes (future collaboration prospects, introductions to other organizations), and whether the research informed broader practices in the field.

Share What You've Learned

Write up the case study for the HAAG community, present findings at conferences and in peer-reviewed venues, make tools, data, or code openly available when possible, and document what you'd do differently next time.

Plan for Continuation or Conclusion

If the partnership is ending, create a transition plan so work isn't lost. If it's continuing, establish the scope and timeline for the next phase. Maintain relationships even after the formal partnership ends — these often become valuable for future work.

Checkpoint: Impact Documentation

Write up the partnership: what was attempted, what was achieved, what was learned. Share with the HAAG community and broader field.

Supporting Resources

Partnership Agreement Checklist

Before signing a formal partnership agreement, confirm the following are addressed:

IP Ownership: Who owns intellectual property generated by the research? Can HAAG publish? Under what conditions?

Data Access & Security: What data is shared? How is it stored, secured, and disposed of at the end?

Confidentiality: What is confidential? For how long? What happens to confidential information at partnership end?

Publication Rights: Can researchers publish findings? When? Do partners have review rights before submission?

Budget & Cost Split: Who pays for what? Is funding from grants, direct payment, or in-kind?

Exit Clauses: What happens if either party needs to exit? How is work handed off?

Points of Contact: Named contacts on both sides with defined roles and availability.

Reporting Requirements: Frequency and format of progress updates to the partner.

Communication Plan Template

Establish this at the start of Phase 4. Revisit at each quarterly review.

Check-in cadence: Monthly (brief, 30 min) and quarterly (deep dive, 60-90 min)

Format: Video call preferred; email for routine updates

HAAG point of contact: [Name, role, email]

Partner point of contact: [Name, role, email]

Shared channel: Email thread, Slack, or shared folder for documents

Progress reporting: Brief written summary after each quarterly review; interim findings shared as they emerge

Escalation path: If issues arise between check-ins, contact the Initiative Lead directly at acooper90@gatech.edu

Impact Documentation Guide

At partnership conclusion (or at each annual renewal), document the following:

Research outputs: Publications, conference presentations, technical reports, code or datasets released

Practical outcomes: Tools deployed, recommendations implemented, processes changed at the partner organization

Capacity built: Skills gained by HAAG team members and new capabilities at the partner organization

Relationship outcomes: Future collaboration prospects and introductions to other organizations

What worked: 2-3 factors most critical to partnership success

What didn't: Honest account of challenges, missteps, or unmet expectations

Lessons for next time: Specific changes to recommend for future HAAG partnerships
