Frequently Asked Questions

Questions from HAAG faculty and researchers about starting and running real-world partnerships through this procedure. Still have questions? Get in touch.

Getting Started

Q: I have a research project that might benefit from a real-world partner. Where do I start?
Start with Phase 1 of the procedure: run the key questions checklist to assess whether your project has genuine partnership potential. If the answer is mostly "yes," reach out to the initiative lead at acooper90@gatech.edu to discuss next steps. If you're not sure, that conversation is still worth having.
Q: My research is theoretical. Does this procedure apply to me?
Probably not directly. This procedure is designed for research with an applied dimension where data access, domain expertise, or operational context would meaningfully improve the work. If your research is purely theoretical, a partnership may not add value and would add overhead. That said, some theoretical work eventually generates applied spin-offs — worth revisiting then.
Q: Do I need a partner already identified to start the procedure?
No. Phase 1 is specifically about assessing whether your research is suited for a partnership. If it is, the Opportunities directory and PIN are good starting points for identifying potential partners. The Charleston partnership originated through PIN's community research matching program.
Q: How is this different from a consulting engagement or a contract research project?
The key distinction is mutual benefit and academic independence. In a consulting engagement, the client defines the deliverable and owns the outcome. In a HAAG partnership, the research question must have genuine academic value, HAAG retains publication rights (with negotiated timelines), and both parties shape the scope together. If a partner wants to fully specify the work product and control the output, that is consulting, not research collaboration.

The Procedure

Q: How long does it typically take to go from first contact to active research?
Roughly 10-12 weeks for a straightforward partnership: 2 weeks of exploratory conversation, 4 weeks of deep scoping, and 4 weeks to finalize agreements and get institutional buy-in. Regulatory complexity, data agreements, or slow institutional review can extend this. See the Partnership Timeline for a phase-by-phase breakdown.
Q: Is the procedure mandatory, or is it a set of guidelines?
The phase structure and Go/No-Go checkpoints are highly encouraged for partnerships operating under this initiative. The specific templates (scope document, communication plan) are defaults that can be adapted to fit the partnership.
Q: Who needs to sign off at each phase?
Phase 1: faculty advisor and initiative lead. Phase 2: faculty advisor, initiative lead, and department leadership. Phase 3: Georgia Tech OTT, HAAG leadership, and the external partner. See Governance: Partnership Approval Levels for the full breakdown.
Q: What does a good scope document look like?
Two to three pages covering: the partner's problem, the specific research questions you'll address, what's in scope and what's not, data and resource requirements, success metrics, team structure, timeline, budget, and communication plan. The Scope Template in Phase 2 walks through each element.
Q: What if the scope changes mid-partnership?
Small adjustments are normal and can be handled at quarterly reviews. Substantial new work should be scoped and approved as a "Phase 2" extension with its own timeline and budget rather than absorbed into the current project. The scope document is a living reference, not a contract carved in stone, but changes need explicit agreement from both sides.

OMSCS & HAAG Context

Q: How does HAAG fit within OMSCS, and what do students get out of participating in a partnership?
HAAG exists specifically to give remote, asynchronous OMSCS students access to real research experience — something traditionally harder to access in an online program. Students contributing to a partnership project work on real data, with a real external stakeholder, producing outputs that go beyond coursework. That experience is directly useful on an academic or professional resume: publications, technical reports, deployed tools, and demonstrated ability to conduct applied research in a collaborative setting.
Q: HAAG researchers are distributed OMSCS students. How does that affect a partnership?
It shapes how the team operates but doesn't limit what's achievable. OMSCS students bring professional backgrounds (many are working practitioners), strong technical skills, and often direct experience with the kinds of problems we research. The distributed model means communication needs to be more deliberate: asynchronous by default, with structured check-ins. The Charleston partnership worked entirely in this model.
Q: Can a faculty member at another GT department run a partnership through this procedure?
The procedure is designed for HAAG-affiliated research, but the framework is general enough that other GT groups could adapt it. If you're outside HAAG and looking for a starting point, you're welcome to use these materials. For formal partnerships carrying the HAAG name, the initiative lead and HAAG leadership need to be involved from Phase 1.

IP, Data & Agreements

Q: Who owns the research outputs?
By default, Georgia Tech retains ownership of intellectual property generated by its researchers. Partners may negotiate licenses, co-ownership of jointly developed tools, or exclusive use windows. Georgia Tech's Office of Technology Transfer handles these agreements. Set expectations early — surprises at Phase 3 are common and avoidable.
Q: Can we publish findings if the partner's data is proprietary?
Yes, but it requires negotiation upfront. Common approaches: publish findings without revealing raw data, allow a partner review window (typically 30-90 days) before submission, use aggregated or anonymized data in publications, or embargo findings for a fixed period. Indefinite publication restrictions are generally incompatible with academic norms — surface this in Phase 2, not Phase 3.
Q: What if the partner wants data analysis done on their infrastructure?
That's workable. On-premise or secure enclave analysis is a standard approach for sensitive data. Discuss this during scoping so the team can plan around access constraints. It adds overhead but is not a blocker.

Funding

Q: Does the partner need to fund the research?
Not necessarily. Some partnerships are grant-funded (NSF CIVIC, NSF S&CC, PIN Community Research grants), some involve direct partner contributions, and some are unfunded but mutually beneficial enough to justify the work. See the Funding Sources directory for specific programs. Bring the funding question into scoping early — it shapes the timeline and what's achievable.
Q: PIN facilitated the Charleston partnership. How does that work exactly?
PIN (Partnership for Inclusive Innovation) runs a Community Research grant program that funds year-long pilot projects pairing GT researchers with local governments. They provide competitive grants, project management support, and a statewide network. You propose a joint project with a government partner; PIN funds and supports the work. This is the most direct on-ramp for HAAG researchers looking for a first community partnership. See Opportunities: Facilitation & Partnership Networks.
Q: What NSF programs are most relevant?
The two strongest fits are the NSF Civic Innovation Challenge (CIVIC), which funds university-community pilots up to $1M for projects with a government partner identified, and NSF Smart and Connected Communities (S&CC), which funds interdisciplinary urban computing research up to $1.5M. Both require a community partner. See Funding Sources for details.

General

Q: I'm interested in running a partnership but don't know where to find a partner. What should I do?
Browse the Opportunities directory by research area. PIN is the fastest path to a facilitated match with a Georgia local government. MetroLab connects GT with city governments nationally. If you have a specific city or organization in mind that isn't listed, reach out to the initiative lead to discuss whether it's a good fit and how to approach them.
Q: I want to take over or continue this initiative. What's involved?
See the Continuation & Handoff section and the Contact page for details. Reach out to acooper90@gatech.edu to arrange a structured handoff — documentation, partner introductions, and open items briefing included.
